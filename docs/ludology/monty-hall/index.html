The Monty Hall Problem

< Back Home

Probability is hard, and humans are famously bad at reasoning aabout it because we are creatures of intuition. And this is a classic example where intuition leads us astray.

Monty Hall was a host on a game show called Let's Make A Deal and was actually not a mathematician. This famous math problem has been around since at least 1975 when it was published in its current form and would be argued over by mathematicians via newspaper editorials, as was the style at the time.

The Situation

Monty presents to you three doors, of which only one contains a prize. You choose a door, and he reveals one of the other two that he knows is not a winning door. He then offers if you would like to switch doors, and you should always say YES!!!

Here's why:

The Math

The door you picked has a 1/3 chance of being the winning door. That also means there's a collective 2/3 chance the prize is NOT behind your door. When Monty reveals the empty door it does not suddenly even out to a 50/50 choice, the 2/3 chance gets concentrated onto the other door.

This means you have 1/3 chance to win a prize if you stay with your first choice, and a 2/3 chance to win if you switch doors. The easiest way to observe this is to explicitly list out the possibilities:

  1. If the first door, the one you pick, started as the winning door then switching is a losing move.
  2. If Door #2 started as the winning door, Monty would reveal Door #3 and switching is a winning move.
  3. If Door #3 started as the winning door, Monty would reveal Door #2 and switching is a winning move.

Switching wins that fancy home dinette two out of three times.

The Psychology

How many people would choose to switch doors? Unless you know how to math out precisely what's happening, it looks like an even 50/50 chance whether you switch or not. There's a very human element at play here: ego.

Many people are prone to keeping their first pick simply because it was their first. They chose it. They've developed an emotional attachment to their selection and remain with it because it was their choice. Switching away can make it appear as though you didn't believe enough in yourself when you made the first selection. But really, you should be thanking Monty as he's offering you twice the chance you started with to win your prize!

I'll leave you with a similar brain teaser: you are speaking to a woman at a park, and she mentions she has two children and at least one is a boy. What are the odds her other child is also a boy?

Hint: Like Monty Hall's doors, the answer depends on how you receive the information.
